# Optical Balancing in Logo/Identity Design: Documented Cases

Research notes for the OPTICAL BALANCING project — Worker C (logo/identity case studies).
Compiled 2026-10-09. Scope: documented cases where designers or studios applied
optical correction (deliberate deviation from geometric/grid precision to achieve
*perceived* balance), with verified specifics. Cases without documentation are
refused and filed under "Unsourced lore" at the end.

Sourcing tiers:
- **Tier 1** = peer-reviewed papers. (Result: none found that document an optical
  correction in a specific commercial logo — see note at end of file.)
- **Tier 2** = documented practitioner sources (designer statements, brand-published
  design documents, first-hand analyses with measurements). Each claim below carries
  its source. All summaries are in my own words; direct quotes are under 50 words.

## Case 1 — Nintendo Switch logo (Nintendo, 2016/2017): right half narrowed

### The claim (verified as a geometric fact)
The Switch logo looks symmetrical but is not. The left Joy-Con is drawn as an
outline; the right Joy-Con is a solid block of color. Because the solid right
half carries more apparent visual weight than the outlined left half, the right
half was drawn *narrower* to keep the two halves perceptually balanced.

### What was corrected, how much, why
- **What:** the solid right Joy-Con is slightly narrower than the outlined left one.
- **How much:** an independent Photoshop analysis measured the difference as
  roughly the line width of the Joy-Con outline — i.e., the right side is
  narrower by exactly the amount of ink its stroke outline does not add
  (sidequesting.com, analysis by editor Dalibor "Dali" Dimovski, March 2017).
  The rationale offered: the red outline plus the white space of the left half
  graphically "average out" to the width of the full-red right half, so equal
  geometric widths would make the solid side look heavier.
- **Why:** so that the viewer's eye is not pulled to one side; the two halves
  should carry equal visual weight.
- **Cross-checks:** game artist David Hellman first documented the asymmetry on
  Twitter on 6 January 2017 with centerline overlays ("Each side has a different
  apparent visual weight, so the logo is 'balanced' by eye…") and a mockup of a
  truly symmetrical version, which looks visibly wrong. Game designer Ian Wexler
  published geometric breakdown diagrams in reply (reported by Mic, 9 Jan 2017).

### Verdict on intent
**Partially verified.** The geometric asymmetry is real and measurable, and the
weight-compensation explanation is consistent with standard logo practice. BUT:
no statement from Nintendo or from the logo's designers has been found
confirming the intent. All intent claims (including mine above) are analyst
inference, not primary-source fact. See "Unsourced lore."

## Case 2 — Google "G" (Google Design, 2015): optically refined, not a true circle

### The claim (verified by the brand's own design document)
Google's own design team explicitly states that the four-color "G" is NOT a
geometrically perfect circle, and explains why.

### What was corrected, how much, why
From Google's *Evolving the Google Identity* design document (Sept 2015), as
quoted in both Adweek and Creative Bloq:
- **Derived** directly from the logotype's "G," with increased visual weight so
  the mark holds up at small sizes and when sharing space with other elements.
- **Built on the same grid** as Google's product iconography.
- **Optically refined to prevent a visual "overbite"** at the point where the
  circular form meets the crossbar — i.e., where the horizontal bar joins the
  ring, a pure geometric circle would make the join look as if the ring bites
  past the bar; the curves were adjusted by eye until the junction reads clean.
- **Color proportions** convey the full spectrum of the logotype and are
  sequenced to aid eye movement around the letterform (so the color breaks are
  also placed for perceptual, not purely geometric, reasons).

No numeric amount (pixels, degrees, percentages) is stated by Google — the
refinement is described qualitatively, as is normal for this kind of correction.

### Corroborating demonstration
A user ("maxt0r") redrew the G as a geometrically perfect circle with evenly
divided color segments; the geometrically "correct" version looked subtly
unbalanced and characterless next to the real logo (reported by Creative Bloq,
"Google logo sparks 'correct design' debate").

## Case 3 — Google wordmark pixel nudge (2014): confirmed one-pixel kerning fix

### What was corrected, how much, why
In May 2014, Redditor nal1200 noticed that Google had shifted its wordmark:
the "g" moved **one pixel to the right** and the "l" moved **one pixel down and
to the right** (the bottom of the "l" and "e" had not lined up horizontally).
Google confirmed through a spokesperson: "we tweaked the logo a little while
ago to make sure it looks its sharpest regardless of your screen resolution"
(Gizmodo, May 2014; repeated in Adweek). A microscopic optical/alignment
correction, acknowledged on the record.

## Case 4 — Starbucks Siren (Lippincott, 2011): deliberate asymmetry for perceived humanity

### What was corrected, how much, why
In the 2011 redesign, Lippincott's team found that a perfectly symmetrical
Siren face looked uncannily inhuman — "a bit creepy… like she was a shell of a
person, like an alien or robot pretending to be a human" (global creative
director Connie Birdsall, quoted in Fast Company). The fix:
- **What:** the shadow on the right side of the Siren's nose was extended —
  her nose dips lower on the right than the left — "the fix of just a few
  pixels that made the Siren work."
- **Why:** to read as human rather than a "perfectly cut mask." As design
  partner Bogdan Geana put it, the slight asymmetry to the face meant "it felt
  a bit more human."

This is optical balancing driven by perception rather than weight: perfect
symmetry produced a worse perceptual result, so the designers broke geometry
deliberately. Note: the intent here is *warmth/imperfection*, not equalizing
visual weight — a different motive from the Switch or Google cases, but the
same mechanism (deviate from geometry, trust the eye).

## Case 5 — Type overshoot (the canonical documented mechanism behind logo corrections)

Not a single logo, but the single best-documented optical-correction mechanism
in the trade, directly applicable to wordmarks and symbols. Every letter drawn
with curves (O, C, S) or points (A, V, W) is drawn measurably taller/deeper
than flat letters (H, E, T) so they *appear* the same height — otherwise curves
recede visually.

### Documented specifics
- **Amounts stated:** typically 1–3% of cap/x-height for "O" (Wikipedia,
  citing standard references); Peter Karow's *Digital Formats for Typefaces*
  recommends **3% for O and 5% for A**.
- **Why (perception mechanism):** type historian Tobias Frere-Jones: "If the
  'correct' height appears inadequate, 'too much' will look right. So the O is
  made taller and deeper than the H… But we read with our eyes, not with
  rulers, so the eye should win every time" (his "Typeface Mechanics" essay,
  via Fast Company, 2014).
- Microsoft researcher Kevin Larson (Advanced Reading Technologies) identified
  the Müller-Lyer illusion as the psychological cause: stems resemble line
  segments with inward chevrons (appear taller) while O resembles outward
  chevrons (appears smaller) (Gizmodo, 2015).
- Jonathan Hoefler (documentary *Abstract: The Art of Design*, materials via
  Fast Company 2019): "Typefaces are born from the struggle between rules and
  results… For the rational mind, type design can be a maddening game of
  drawing things differently in order to make them appear the same."
- General principle for logos (Pangram Pangram, 2026): circles paired with
  squares are drawn slightly larger than the squares; horizontal strokes are
  drawn lighter than vertical ones of the same nominal weight, because
  horizontal lines appear heavier.

## Case 6 — Twitter bird (2012): a geometry-first counter-example, adjusted by eye

Included as contrast, because it is often cited in the same breath as the
Google G but represents the opposite approach.

- The 2012 Twitter bird (by Martin Grasser with two other designers) is
  constructed purely from three sets of overlapping circles — a geometric
  construction, documented by Twitter's then-creative director Doug Bowman in
  the launch statement (via Dezeen/iclarified, June 2012).
- Grasser himself (Decrypt/Digiday, July 2023) describes the process as a blend:
  "it's my responsibility to adjust them on a two-dimensional surface until they
  feel just right — a blend of mathematics and artistic intuition."
- Lesson for the project: the bird's charm comes from commitment to one
  geometric system, not from optical correction. Geometric construction and
  optical correction are two distinct (sometimes opposing) strategies.

## Unsourced lore — claims I refused to assert

1. **Nintendo's intent on the Switch logo.** No Nintendo or designer statement
   confirming deliberate optical balancing has been located. The asymmetry is
   real; the *intent* is analyst inference (plausible, consistent with
   practice). Any source that states intent as fact (e.g., Fabrik Brands'
   "Nintendo didn't make the controllers different sizes by accident" or the
   yin-yang symbolism framing) is speculating. Treat all such claims as
   UNVERIFIED.

2. **Spotify's pause button "1 pixel off-center."** In 2018, Redditor sqrtoftwo
   measured the pause/play glyphs as off-center (1px desktop; 2px and 0.5px
   variants on iOS) and speculated it might be intentional; commenter
   CarolWendelson guessed "It was probably aligned optically… this is super
   common." No Spotify designer or official source has confirmed or denied.
   This is anecdote + speculation, NOT a documented correction. (ShortList,
   https://www.shortlist.com/news/spotify-pause-button-technology-reddit-design)

3. **Apple logo golden-ratio construction — DEBUNKED.** The viral
   circles-and-golden-rectangle diagrams are retro-fitted lore. Original
   designer Rob Janoff: "When I designed it I pretty much did it freehand";
   the geometric refinements came from Landor Associates in the early 1980s,
   who made the shapes "much more symmetrical, much more geometric"
   (interview quoted in Gizmodo). Quora product designer David Cole measured
   the ratios across the mark at 1.53–1.73 — not 1.618 — and concluded: "Real
   visual rhythm is hurt by precision… if it looks right, it is right"
   (via Fast Company, "Debunking The Myth Of Apple's 'Golden Ratio'").
   Apple uses slightly different logo geometries in different official
   materials. Lesson: many "geometric construction" diagrams online are
   post-hoc decoration, which is exactly why this project's sourcing standard
   matters. Sources: https://www.FastCompany.com/1672682/debunking-the-myth-of-apple-s-golden-ratio ;
   https://gizmodo.com/does-the-apple-logo-really-adhere-to-the-golden-ratio-511410550

4. **Pepsi "BREATHTAKING" rationale document (Arnell Group, 2008).** The leaked
   27-page PDF claims the globe was derived from the golden ratio, the Mona
   Lisa, the Parthenon, Earth's magnetic fields, etc. It is a real leaked
   document (archived: https://archive.org/details/breathtaking-design-strategy-pepsi-gravitational-field-arnell-group-august-4-2008),
   but its authorship and authenticity have been questioned — some believe it
   is a hoax (per Internet Archive summary and Creative Bloq,
   https://www.creativebloq.com/news/pepsi-document-ridiculous). Either way it
   is geometric *mystification*, not documented optical correction — useful as
   a cautionary exhibit, not evidence.

5. **Airbnb Bélo, Mastercard 2016, Audi rings.** Their construction documents
   exist (DesignStudio, Pentagram/Bierut, Audi's Georgi & Kalisch interviews)
   but none documents a geometric-vs-optical *correction* with specifics; the
   Audi "ring diameter : gap = 10:1" type claims found online trace to a
   low-quality SEO page, not an Audi source. Excluded rather than stretched.

6. **Instagram 2016 icon (Ian Spalter).** Process documented (Medium post,
   Fast Company) but no optical-correction specifics stated. Excluded.

## Tier 1 note

No peer-reviewed paper was found that documents an optical correction in a
*specific* commercial logo or identity. Peer-reviewed literature that *is*
directly relevant sits at the mechanism level: geometric/optical illusions of
size and alignment (Müller-Lyer, irradiation, horizontal–vertical illusion —
the same illusions Kevin Larson connects to type overshoot) and logo-symmetry
perception studies (e.g., "Beauty of symmetry — The impact of logo symmetry on
perceived product quality," PLOS ONE, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0317229;
Journal of Marketing studies on perceptual structure in brand marks). This is a
gap in the academic record worth stating in the research: practitioners
document corrections in trade/brand sources, but the academic literature has
not, to my knowledge, published a case study of a specific logo's optical
correction with measurements.

## Takeaways for the research

1. **Best-documented case overall: Google's G.** The brand itself used the
   phrase "optically refined" and named the exact junction corrected (the
   circle–crossbar "overbite"). No numeric amount given — typical of
   practitioner sources; corrections are described qualitatively.
2. **Only hard numbers found:** Spotify's 1px (unconfirmed), Google's 1px
   wordmark nudge (confirmed), Switch's ~1 stroke-width narrowing
   (amateur-measured), overshoot percentages from type practice (1–5%).
   Practitioners rarely publish measurements — a methodological finding in
   itself.
3. **Two distinct motives for breaking geometry:** (a) weight/equilibrium
   balance (Switch, Google G, overshoot); (b) perceptual/human quality
   (Starbucks — symmetry looked *worse*). The research hypothesis about
   pixel-group "weight" covers (a); (b) suggests the model needs a
   human-likeness/uncanny-valley term too.
4. **The field is polluted with post-hoc geometry lore** (Apple golden ratio,
   Pepsi "Breathtaking"), which makes primary-source discipline the main
   contribution of this worker's strand.
