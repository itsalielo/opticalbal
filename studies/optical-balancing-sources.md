# Optical Balancing — Academic Source Landscape & Adjacent Literature

Worker F report · 2026-10-09 · for the optical-balancing research project (perceived visual balance vs geometric alignment).

**Scope.** PART 1 is the practical source landscape: where a design researcher would actually go to build this project — journals, vision/perception venues, thesis repositories, databases, archives, in several languages. PART 2 is the adjacent literature that illuminates how visual weight works, even where nobody says "optical balance" — colour theory, attention/saliency, neuroaesthetics, empirical aesthetics, visual hierarchy. The two parts are kept strictly separate so the core bibliography stays clean.

**Sourcing discipline.** Everything below was verified to exist via web search on 2026-10-09. Every journal, repository, database and paper carries a link and/or DOI plus an access-status note. Items I could not verify are explicitly flagged **UNVERIFIED** and never presented as literature. No invented citations.

## How the tiers work

- **Tier 1** — peer-reviewed journals, peer-reviewed conference proceedings, doctoral theses. Carries evidential weight.
- **Tier 2** — informed non-academic sources: classic practitioner books, archives, institutional pages, practitioner studies. Clearly marked; never stands in for Tier 1.
- Summaries are in my own words. Direct quotation, where used, stays well under 50 words per work.

## 1.2 Vision & perception journals (the "other half" of the problematic)

Optical balancing sits exactly between design and vision science. These are the peer-reviewed venues where the perception side is published — most of them open access, which is convenient.

- **Journal of Vision** — Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO). **Fully open access, online-only**, peer-reviewed; all aspects of functional vision (spatial vision, colour, attention, eye movements). Index
ed in MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science. The single best OA venue for vision-science mechanics.
  - http://jov.arvojournals.org/ss/about.aspx
- **i-Perception** — SAGE, **open access** (CC BY, APC with waiver policy), peer-reviewed; perception science broadly. Several of the adjacent-literature papers below are i-Perception articles — it's where short, sharp perception studies land.
  - DOAJ: https://doaj.org/toc/2041-6695
- **Perception** — SAGE (formerly Pion), ISSN 0301-0066. The classic perception journal. Subscription.
- **Vision Research** — Elsevier. Subscription; publishes colour, spatial vision and aesthetic-vision studies (e.g. Graham et al. 2014 on luminance histograms).
- **Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics** — Springer/Psychonomic Society. Subscription; key for attention and colour-combination aesthetics (e.g. Schloss & Palmer 2011).

- **PQDT Open (ProQuest)** — open-access full-text US dissertations and theses, free to read.
- **theses.fr / TEL (Thèses en ligne)** — French doctoral theses (theses.fr) and full-text via HAL's TEL; in French. Via https://theses.fr and https://hal.science

  - http://www.diva-portal.org
- **Theses Canada** — Library and Archives Canada; 300,000+ Canadian theses.
- **Trove (National Library of Australia)** — Australian/NZ full-text theses.

## 1.4 Databases, discovery tools, directories

In the order a working researcher would actually use them:

1. **Google Scholar** (https://scholar.google.com) — the broadest net, best citation chaining ("cited by"). First stop for any paper.
2. **Semantic Scholar** (https://semanticscholar.org) — AI-powered literature search, 200M+ papers; TLDR summaries, citation contexts, "influential citations" filter. Best for mapping a subfield quickly.
3. **OpenAlex** (https://openalex.org) — free, open knowledge graph of 200M+ scholarly works by the nonprofit OurResearch; the open successor to Microsoft Academic Graph. Has a free API; excellent for systematic searches and for finding which venues publish on a topic.
4. **BASE – Bielefeld Academic Search Engine** (https://www.base-search.net) — one of the world's largest academic search engines (240M+ documents, ~60% OA full text); OA results ranked first and filterable. The Bielefeld University Library runs it.
5. **CORE** (https://core.ac.uk) — aggregates open-access research papers from repositories and journals worldwide (180M+); also finds legal OA versions of paywalled papers.
6. **DOAJ – Directory of Open Access Journals** (https://doaj.org) — the authority for verifying that a journal is genuinely open access. Use it to vet any OA venue before citing or submitting.
7. **DOAB – Directory of Open Access Books** (https://www.doab.org) — for OA monographs (useful for Arnheim-era and aesthetics books).
8. **Crossref / OpenCitations** — DOI resolution and open citation data (https://www.crossref.org).
9. **Unpaywall** (https://unpaywall.org) — finds legal free versions of paywalled articles; browser extension.
10. **Internet Archive Scholar** (https://scholar.archive.org) — 25M+ articles and datasets; good for older/disappeared content.
11. **JURN** (https://jurn.org) — curated index of OA arts/humanities journals.
12. **WorldCat** (https://www.worldcat.org) — global library catalogue; locates physical holdings of journals and books.

# PART 2 — ADJACENT LITERATURE: HOW VISUAL WEIGHT WORKS

> **This section is deliberately separate from the core bibliography.** These works illuminate the mechanisms underneath optical balancing — attention, saliency, colour weight, aesthetic judgment — even where the term "optical balance" never appears. Filed here so the core bibliography (perception science · computational models · Gestalt/Arnheim lineage · typography) stays clean.

## Tier 1 — peer-reviewed adjacent literature

### A. Visual attention & computational saliency

These are the papers behind "the eye goes here first" — the closest experimental analogue to a designer's intuition about visual weight.

1. **Itti, L., Koch, C., & Niebur, E. (1998).** A model of saliency-based visual attention for rapid scene analysis. *IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 20*(11), 1254–1259. https://doi.org/10.1109/34.730558 — **Paywalled (IEEE); author PDF widely hosted.** The founding computational saliency model: multiscale colour, intensity and orientation features fused into one topographic saliency map, with a winner-take-all network selecting locations in decreasing saliency order. In design terms: a formalisation of "what pops", decomposable by feature channel — a direct ancestor of the factor-decomposition hypothesis (weight = saturation, hue, lightness, area, position, contrast).
2. **Itti, L., & Koch, C. (2001).** Computational modelling of visual attention. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2*(3), 194–203. https://doi.org/10.1038/35058500 — **Paywalled.** Review consolidating the saliency-map framework and five key findings, including that the perceptual saliency of a stimulus critically depends on its surrounding context — the experimental cousin of "balance is relational, not absolute."
3. **Harel, J., Koch, C., & Perona, P. (2007).** Graph-based visual saliency. In *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 19 (NIPS 2006)* (pp. 545–552). MIT Press. — **Proceedings; freely available.** A graph-theoretic reformulation of saliency (activation maps as Markov chains), more biologically plausible than Itti–Koch and widely reimplemented. Shows the saliency idea surviving a change of mathematical machinery — relevant to the "exact formula" ambition.
4. **Henderson, J. M., & Hayes, T. R. (2017).** Meaning-based guidance of attention in scenes as revealed by meaning maps. *Nature Human Behaviour, 1*(10), 743–747. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0208-0 — **Paywalled.** Demonstrated that *meaning*, not just low-level salience, guides fixations — the main corrective to pure bottom-up models. For design: visual weight has a semantic component that no pixel-formula can absorb without a meaning layer.
5. **Itti, L., & Koch, C. (2000).** A saliency-based search mechanism for overt and covert shifts of visual attention. *Vision Research, 40*(10–12), 1489–1506. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0042-6989(99)00163-7 — **Paywalled.** Earlier full exposition of the search mechanism; pairs with the 1998 paper.
6. **Mackworth, N. H., & Morandi, A. J. (1967).** The gaze selects informative details within pictures. *Perception & Psychophysics, 2*(11), 547–552. https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03210264 — **Historical Tier 1.** The original demonstration that fixations cluster on informative regions — the empirical seed of all saliency work.

### B. Empirical aesthetics — how preference and judgment actually behave

7. **Leder, H., Belke, B., Oeberst, A., & Augustin, D. (2004).** A model of aesthetic appreciation and aesthetic judgments. *British Journal of Psychology, 95*, 489–508. https://doi.org/10.1348/0007126042369811 — **Paywalled.** The dominant information-processing model of aesthetic experience: five stages from perceptual analysis through cognitive mastering to evaluation, with perceiver variables (expertise, taste) modulating every stage. The standard framework any balance-and-preference claim must position itself against. Follow-up: **Leder, H., & Nadal, M. (2014).** Ten years of a model of aesthetic appreciation and aesthetic judgments. *British Journal of Psychology, 105*, 443–464. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjop.12084
8. **Reber, R., Schwarz, N., & Winkielman, P. (2004).** Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure: Is beauty in the perceiver's processing experience? *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 8*(4), 364–382. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3 — **Paywalled; author manuscript on SSRN.** Argues aesthetic pleasure tracks how *fluently* the perceiver processes the object — symmetry, contrast, prototypicality all feed fluency. Directly relevant: a balanced composition may please not because of its geometry but because balance makes processing easy. A serious alternative hypothesis for the problematic.
9. **Palmer, S. E., Schloss, K. B., & Sammartino, J. (2013).** Visual aesthetics and human preference. *Annual Review of Psychology, 64*, 77–107. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-120710-100504 — **Paywalled.** The field-defining review: methods, findings on colour and spatial composition preferences, and the competing theoretical accounts (mere exposure, arousal, prototypes, ecological valence, fluency). The mandatory orientation document.
10. **Jacobsen, T., & Höfel, L. (2003).** Descriptive and evaluative judgment processes: Behavioral and electrophysiological indices of processing symmetry and aesthetics. *Cognitive, Affective, & Behavioral Neuroscience, 3*, 289–299. https://doi.org/10.3758/CABN.3.4.289 — **Paywalled.** ERP evidence that symmetry is processed fast and feeds aesthetic evaluation; companion behavioural paper: **Jacobsen, T., & Höfel, L. (2002).** Aesthetic judgments of novel graphic patterns. *Perceptual and Motor Skills, 95*, 755–766. https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.2002.95.3.755
11. **Leder, H., Tinio, P. P. L., Brieber, D., Kröner, T., Jacobsen, T., & Rosenberg, R. (2018).** Symmetry is not a universal law of beauty. *Empirical Studies of the Arts, 37*(1), 104–114. https://doi.org/10.1177/0276237418777941 — **Paywalled.** The counterpoint: symmetry's beauty effect is conditional, not lawlike — a useful brake on over-mathematising balance.
12. **Preference for symmetry, balance, or proximity in picture aesthetics depends on the method of evaluation (2025).** *PMC.* https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12638637/ — **Open access.** Recent evidence that *how you ask* changes whether balance predicts liking — methodologically important for any experiment he designs.
13. **Chatterjee, A., & Vartanian, O. (2014).** Neuroaesthetics. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 18*(7), 370–375. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2014.03.003 — **Paywalled.** Compact state-of-the-field: the neural systems of aesthetic experience (sensory-motor, emotion-valuation, meaning-knowledge). Companion: **Chatterjee, A. (2011).** Neuroaesthetics: A coming of age story. *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience, 23*(1), 53–62. https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2010.21457 — **Paywalled.** And the wider review: **Pearce, M. T., Zaidel, D. W., Vartanian, O., Skov, M., Leder, H., Chatterjee, A., & Nadal, M. (2016).** Neuroaesthetics: The cognitive neuroscience of aesthetic experience. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11*, 265–279.
14. **Chatterjee, A., Widick, P., Sternschein, R., Smith, W. B., & Bromberger, B. (2010).** The assessment of art attributes. *Empirical Studies of the Arts, 28*(2), 207–222. https://doi.org/10.2190/EM.28.2.f — **Paywalled.** How viewers decompose artworks into attributes (balance, colour, complexity among them) — a behavioural precedent for the factor-decomposition hypothesis.

### C. Colour as a weight factor

15. **Palmer, S. E., & Schloss, K. B. (2010).** An ecological valence theory of human color preference. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 107*(19), 8877–8882. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0906172107 — **Open access (PNAS).** Colour preference tracks the average affective valence of objects associated with that colour — the strongest current account of *why* colours carry different psychological weight. Foundational for any saturation/hue/lightness weighting scheme.
16. **Schloss, K. B., & Palmer, S. E. (2011).** Aesthetic response to color combinations: Preference, harmony, and similarity. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 73*, 551–571. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-010-0027-0 — **Paywalled.** How colour pairs are judged — preference, harmony and similarity dissociate; figure–ground assignment of the pair matters. Directly relevant to balancing coloured elements against each other.
17. **Wilms, L., & Oberfeld, D. (2018).** Color and emotion: Effects of hue, saturation, and brightness. *Psychological Research, 82*(5), 896–918. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-017-0880-8 — **Paywalled.** Disentangles the separate emotional contributions of hue, saturation and brightness — empirical support for treating them as independent weight factors.

### D. Balance-specific and design-adjacent experimental work

18. **Locher, P., Gray, S., & Nodine, C. (1996).** The structural framework of pictorial balance. *Perception, 25*(12), 1419–1436. https://doi.org/10.1068/p251419 — **Paywalled.** Two experiments on how subjective balance is created by structural features of paintings; participants located balance centres, assigned weights to pictorial features, and rated balance — the closest experimental ancestor of the optical-balancing project.
19. **Locher, P. J., Stappers, P. J., & Overbeeke, K. (1998).** The role of balance as an organizing design principle underlying adults' compositional strategies for creating visual displays. *Acta Psychologica, 99*(2), 141–161. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0001-6918(98)00008-0 — **Paywalled.** Balance as an *organising principle people actually use when making displays* — the design-side mirror of the perception studies.
20. **Locher, P. J., Cornelis, E., Wagemans, J., & Stappers, P. J. (2001).** Artists' use of compositional balance for creating visual displays. *Empirical Studies of the Arts, 19*(2), 213–227. https://doi.org/10.2190/ekmd-ymn5-njug-34bk — **Paywalled.** How trained artists deploy balance compositionally.
21. **Wilson, A., & Chatterjee, A. (2005).** The assessment of preference for balance: Introducing a new test. *Empirical Studies of the Arts, 23*, 165–180. — **Paywalled; no DOI located.** Introduces the **APB (Assessment of Preference for Balance)** index: a mathematically defined balance score computed from pixel distributions of geometric shapes across the vertical, horizontal and two diagonal axes plus inner/outer regions (building on Arnheim's structural skeleton), validated against human balance and preference ratings. The single most on-point precedent for a quantitative balance formula.
22. **Hübner, R., & Fillinger, M. G. (2016).** Comparison of objective measures for predicting perceptual balance and visual aesthetic preference. *Frontiers in Psychology, 7*, 335. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00335 — **Open access.** Head-to-head test of competing objective balance metrics (APB, DCM, mirror symmetry, homogeneity) against human balance ratings and liking of abstract patterns. Directly answers "which formula best predicts *perceived* balance" — essential reading for the problematic.
23. **Gershoni, S., & Hochstein, S. (2011).** Measuring pictorial balance perception at first glance using Japanese calligraphy. *i-Perception.* https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485800/ — **Open access.** Balance perceived within the first fixation depends on *different* visual features than the APB index captures — evidence that early (gist) balance and deliberate balance judgments use different feature sets.
24. **Leyssen, M. H., Linsen, S., Sammartino, J., & Palmer, S. E. (2012).** Aesthetic preference for spatial composition in multiobject pictures. *i-Perception.* https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485813/ — **Open access.** Balance effects on preference in multi-object compositions (not just abstract patterns).
25. **Braun, D. I., Hofmann, M., & Doerschner, K. (2026).** Being K. Malevich: A hands-on approach to compositional preference. *i-Perception.* https://doi.org/10.1177/20416695261421103 — **Open access.** Participants *make* compositions (hands-on paradigm) rather than rating them — a methodological model worth stealing.
26. **Bauerly, M., & Liu, Y. (2006).** Computational modeling and experimental investigation of effects of compositional elements on interface and design aesthetics. *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 64*(8), 670–682. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581906000048 — **Paywalled.** Computational quantification of symmetry, balance and element quantity validated against human aesthetic judgments of geometric images *and* realistic web pages — the rare paper that carries balance metrics into actual design artefacts.

### UNVERIFIED (do not cite as literature until checked)

- **Ngo, D. C. L., Teo, L. S., & Byrne, J. G. (2003).** Aesthetic measures for screen design (reported as a 14-measure mathematical model of interface aesthetics). Frequently cited via Bauerly & Liu (2006); I did not independently verify the reference — treat as a lead, not a citation.
- **Reinecke, K., et al. (2013).** Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics (reported DOI 10.1145/2470654.2481281, CHI 2013). Seen only in secondary citation; verify before use.
- **Moshagen, M., & Thielsch, M. T. (2010/2013).** VisAWI — Visual Aesthetics of Websites Inventory. Seen only in secondary citation; verify before use.

## Tier 2 — informed non-academic sources (clearly marked)

These are practitioner books, archives and explainers. They carry design wisdom, not evidential weight — never substitutes for Tier 1.

- **Arnheim, R. (1974).** *Art and Visual Perception: A Psychology of the Creative Eye* (new version). University of California Press. — The book that gave designers the vocabulary of *visual weight*, the structural skeleton of the square, and perceptual forces. Every balance index above (APB included) is built on its framework.
- **Lidwell, W., Holden, K., & Butler, J. (2010).** *Universal Principles of Design* (2nd ed.). Rockport Publishers. — Encyclopaedia of design principles with cited research pointers per principle (aesthetic-usability effect, figure–ground, rule of thirds…); useful as an index into Tier 1, not as evidence itself.
- **Lupton, E., & Phillips, J. C. (2015).** *Graphic Design: The New Basics* (2nd ed.). Princeton Architectural Press. — Chapters on rhythm and balance, hierarchy, grids and Gestalt principles: the practitioner articulation of the problematic.
- **Nielsen, J., & Pernice, K. (2010).** *Eyetracking Web Usability*. New Riders. — Large-scale professional eye-tracking study of how eyes move over page layouts: the practitioner face of visual hierarchy research.
- **Berlyne, D. E. (1971).** *Aesthetics and Psychobiology*. Appleton-Century-Crofts. — The arousal/complexity tradition in empirical aesthetics; historical Tier 2.
- **Monoskop** (https://monoskop.org) and its **Design research** catalogue page (https://monoskop.org/Design_research) — open archive of design texts and journal history. Tier 2.
- **GitHub implementations of the Itti–Koch model** (e.g. https://github.com/lufen147/itti-saliency-based-visual-attention) — working code of the 1998 saliency model; useful for experimenting, not for citing.

## Practical reading/workflow plan (where he would actually go)
