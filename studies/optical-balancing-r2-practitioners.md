## 1. Type designers on optical correction (new voices)

### 1.1 Tim Ahrens & Shoko Mugikura — *Size-specific Adjustments to Type Designs*
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (practitioner book) · **Link:**
https://justanotherfoundry.com/size-specific-adjustments-to-type-designs
(verified live; reprint planned later 2026)

The single most systematic practitioner formalization of optical sizing found in
Round 2. Originated as Ahrens' 2007 MA thesis at Reading, published by Mark Batty
2008, expanded second edition co-authored with Mugikura (Just Another Foundry,
2014). What it contributes:

- **A formalized rule set for size-conditional correction.** The book's stated
  principle: "Characters intended for use in small sizes typically show an
  increased width and x-height, reduced stroke contrast and looser spacing."
  The "Design advice" section systematizes corrections across weight, stroke
  contrast, width, vertical proportions, counters, feature suppression/emphasis,
  serifs, joins, spacing, and interpolation order — i.e., optical correction
  organized as an engineering checklist, not lore.
- **A practitioner interview corpus.** Ahrens interviewed Robert Slimbach,
  David Berlow, Akira Kobayashi, and Christian Schwartz on their size-specific
  practices; their answers plus analyses of old-master and contemporary faces
  form the book's evidence base. (New names for the project's radar: Slimbach,
  Berlow, Kobayashi, Schwartz as witnesses on optical sizing — their interview
  answers live inside this book.)
- **The documentation-gap thesis.** The book's own explanation of *why*
  practitioner quantification is scarce: "From the metal type era, hardly any
  documentation on the subject is available since punchcutting, like other
  crafts, was not discussed much in writing. The skills and insights were passed
  on from one master to the next by demonstration." This is the craft-side root
  cause of Round 1's "practitioners almost never publish measurements" finding.
- Christian Schwartz's foreword states the anti-formula position from inside
  the craft: "there isn't one perfect solution that we're all working towards,
  but rather a range of correct answers, each appropriate to its own situation."

**Contribution to understanding:** the closest the trade has to a *method* for
optical correction — directional rules (increase X, reduce Y) rather than
coefficients, and an explicit claim that no single optimum exists.

### 1.2 TypeDrawers forum — practitioner-measured overshoot values
**Status:** PRACTITIONER-COMMUNITY (named designers participating; Thomas
Phinney posts in-thread) · **Links:**
- https://typedrawers.com/discussion/4060/three-imprints (measured statistics)
- https://typedrawers.com/discussion/2111/too-much-overshoot (rule of thumb)
- http://typedrawers.com/discussion/1012/overshoot-and-slab-serifs (eye doctrine)
- https://typedrawers.com/discussion/4432/metric-and-em-size (unit values)
- https://typedrawers.com/discussion/3993/ (tips & tricks; optical-correction doctrine)

This is where practitioners actually publish numbers — not in case studies, in
forum threads. Findings:

- **Measured overshoot in shipping fonts** ("Three Imprints" thread): a
  practitioner computed bottom-overshoot statistics normalized to EM 100 for
  Helvetica, Arial, Geneva, Verdana. Helvetica: 32 round/pointed glyphs
  (a b c d e o s t u C G J O S U ä ö ü Ö Ü ß 0 3 5 6 8 9 % / æ Œ œ) sit at −2
  units below baseline while 42 flat glyphs sit at −0; Arial shows the same
  two-tier structure (−1 vs −0/+0); Verdana splits rounds into two tiers
  (−2 and −1). I.e., the overshoot is real, measurable, and stratified by
  glyph class in production fonts — a practitioner-published measurement set.
- **Rule-of-thumb ranges:** "General notion is 3%–8% overshoot" (display
  context); "the letter O has an overshoot (2–4% of x-height)" (text faces);
  "around 4 to 10 units (on 1000 UPM)… for rounded terminals in an uppercase
  'A', around 4 units undershoot looks fine, whereas in case of an 'o', it may
  go up to 10 units."
- **In-thread designer numbers:** a practitioner reports his own values —
  "/o overshoots and undershoots… 9 units (1000 UPM). My x-height is 570, so /o
  is a hair more than 3% taller"; "Round capitals overshoot and undershoot 16
  units"; "Vertical stem thickness on capitals is 140 units, horizontal 136"
  — and asks what value others recommend for horizontals. Thomas Phinney
  answers in the same thread.
- **The eye-judgment doctrine, stated by practitioners:** "Don't measure or
  think about percentages. Increase the overshoot until it's noticeable, than
  back it off a bit"; "the Thin weight of a typeface might require almost no
  overshoot, whereas the Fat might need lots"; "Don't get stuck solely on the
  exact math or geometry of shapes, it's sometimes necessary and better to make
  optical corrections that look and feel right more than they are following a
  precise measurement… there is freedom to break the 'grid' when needed";
  "Know the severe limits of the grid, transcending it with optics sooner
  rather than later."

**Contribution to understanding:** the only public venue where working type
designers attach numbers to corrections — and simultaneously the venue where
they articulate *why* the numbers are provisional (weight-dependent,
size-dependent, judged by eye). Both halves matter for the formula question.

### 1.3 Sofie Beier — *Type Tricks: Your Personal Guide to Type Design* (BIS, 2017)
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (practitioner book; contents not inspected — lead)
· **Link:** https://www.bispublishers.com/type-tricks.html (verified live;
ISBN 9789063694586)

~200 pocket-format rules "about typographical rules and the underlying
structure of the work process in the design of new typefaces… both a reference
book and a user manual." The New Design Magazine review notes it covers "how
to look at positive and negative shape as well as how to adjust the shape
perceptually to achieve the proportions intended." Note the dual status: Beier
is also a Tier 1 author in Round 1 (legibility research); this book is her
Tier 2 practitioner codification. **Next step for a future round:** mine its
~200 tricks for quantified correction rules — it is the most likely single
book to contain practitioner numbers beyond Cheng.

### 1.4 Fábio Duarte Martins — Optician Sans "optical corrections"
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (designer's own email to press) · **Link:**
https://gizmodo.com/meet-optician-sans-the-weird-and-wonderful-font-insp-1831107185
(verified)

Martins, the type designer on ANTI Hamar's Optician Sans (a display face
derived from LogMAR eye-chart optotypes), told Gizmodo: "We took the LogMAR
optotypes and did what in type design we call 'optical corrections,' or small
adjustments to make their form aesthetically pleasing." Small case, but it is
a practitioner using the field's own term for the practice on the record, with
the rationale stated: the source forms were engineered for measurement (equal
stroke widths), and had to be *de-engineered* by eye for aesthetic use.

### 1.5 Kris Sowersby (Klim Type Foundry) — black/white balance as the test activity
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (interview) · **Link:**
https://www.creativebloq.com/computer-arts/kris-sowersby-6099053 (verified)

On his working method: "He tests the composition of his work all the time; the
black and white balance in a typeface is crucial. It's what he focuses on
during most of the testing — balancing the spacing, counters, ligatures and so
forth." A working designer's description of optical balancing as the *central*
ongoing activity (not a finishing step): equilibrium of black and white,
assessed continuously in proofing.

## 2. Logo/identity designers on optical correction (new cases)

### 2.1 ★ NBC peacock 2022 redraw — the strongest new case
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (NBC's own creative team, on the record) ·
**Link:**
https://www.newscaststudio.com/2022/12/07/nbc-new-peacock-logo/?og=1
(verified; statements via email interview with Juliet Garrett, SVP NBC
Creative Design, NBCUniversal Television and Streaming; redraw executed with
Sibling Rivalry)

NBC's team described the 2022 peacock redraw to NewscastStudio with unusual
explicitness:

- "The space between those feathers was also **'balanced' out**" — the brand's
  own word for the correction.
- The "beak" of the peacock in the purple feather "was extended slightly to
  make it more prominent."
- NBC acknowledged the peacock "has had a variety of different spacings in
  the past 'to ensure (the) best look in any given case,' such as if
  scalability was an issue" — spacing as a perceptual, context-dependent
  judgment across versions.
- The accompanying logotype (NBC Tinker, by Capacity Studios) was made "bolder
  and with slightly wider spacing between letters" because the previous
  lettering's "stroke widths didn't quite mesh with the shapes and spacing of
  the peacock icon" — and "the thicker lettering appears to mesh well with
  both the more generous spacing between feathers and the relatively heavy
  amount of **visual weight** their solid color portions take up."

**Why this is the find of the round:** a maker-attributed account that uses
the research's own vocabulary ("balanced," "visual weight") and documents a
*symbol–wordmark weight-matching* task — stroke widths and spacing tuned so
two different elements carry compatible visual weight. Same task family as the
Airbnb case below; a candidate sub-problem for the weight function.

### 2.2 Airbnb Bélo lockup refinement — Brian Chesky's walkthrough
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED via SECONDHAND-REPORT (Chesky's own presentation
in a viral video, as reported by NDTV; original talk context not independently
verified) · **Link:**
https://www.ndtv.com/travel/ocd-on-different-levels-internet-reacts-unfavourably-to-airbnb-s-logo-change-8575947/amp/1
(verified live)

In the circulating video, Chesky walks through printouts of the Bélo/icon +
"airbnb" wordmark refinement: the original wordmark appeared "too thick"
while the icon was "too thin"; the team "refined the proportions, aligning the
curvature radius of both elements and resolving spacing inconsistencies" so
the two would "fit harmoniously within a visual triangle" — "from adjusting
letter weights and correcting angles to refining the dot on the letter 'i'
for better visual appeal," ending in "a logo that feels balanced."

**Contribution:** a second maker-attested symbol–wordmark weight-equalization
task, with the specific levers named (stroke weight, curvature radius,
spacing, the i-dot). Caveat: reported secondhand; treat the video's staging
(an internal design talk) as unverified, the described corrections as
Chesky-attributed.

### 2.3 Lufthansa crane 2018 — thinning for digital
**Status:** COMPANY-ATTRIBUTED (Lufthansa via Adweek) · **Link:**
https://www.adweek.com/brand-marketing/why-lufthansa-decided-to-give-its-planes-a-new-paint-job/
(verified)

On the Martin et Karczinski / Ronald Wild redraw: "the crane itself has been
redrawn with thinner lines to make it 'slimmer and fit for the digital
world,' and by reducing the thickness of the ring that encircles the bird,
designers gave the crane more space and an overall 'lightness and elegance'."
The tweaks "were made principally to help the crane show up better on small
digital screens." A documented *weight rebalancing* (stroke thinning +
counterspace reallocation) driven by a perceptual target (legibility/presence
at small sizes), attributed to the company rather than the individual
designer.

### 2.4 Lindon Leader on the FedEx arrow — spacing as perceptual judgment
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (Leader's 2004 The Sneeze interview, via Retail
Brew) · **Link:**
https://www.retailbrew.com/stories/2022/10/07/logo-big-or-go-home-how-fedex-s-secret-arrow-was-created-accidentally

an optical-correction case proper.

## 3. Craft formalizations — rules of thumb with numbers, and tools

### 3.1 Ahrens & Mugikura's design-advice systematics (§1.1)
Directional formalization of optical sizing (small sizes → +width, +x-height,
−contrast, looser spacing), organized by letter-shape parameter and spacing.
Rules, not coefficients — the trade's preferred level of formalization.

### 3.2 Adobe InDesign — Optical Kerning & Optical Margin Alignment
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (vendor documentation) · **Links:**
https://helpx.adobe.com/indesign/using/kerning-tracking.html and
https://helpx.adobe.com/ca/indesign/using/formatting-paragraphs.html
(verified live)

Two shipped algorithms that formalize the designer's eye:

- **Optical kerning:** "adjusts spacing based on the shape of adjacent
  characters rather than predefined font data" — i.e., an algorithmic
  approximation of perceptual spacing judgment, used when the designer's
  kern pairs are absent or insufficient.
- **Optical Margin Alignment:** "controls whether punctuation marks (such as
  periods, commas, quotation marks, and dashes) and edges of letters (such as
  W and A) hang outside the text margins, so that the type looks aligned."
  The software decides per-glyph overhang from glyph shape — hanging
  punctuation and edge compensation as a computed formalization.

**Contribution:** the closest thing in production software to w(pixel group)
for spacing/alignment — shape-driven, per-glyph, and explicitly framed as
making type *look* aligned rather than *be* aligned.

### 3.3 Optical margin alignment — community-compiled suggested values
**Status:** PRACTITIONER-COMMUNITY (compiled table; secondary source) ·
**Link:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_margin_alignment (verified)

The article's "Suggested values" table for common seriffed faces (Times,
Palatino, Garamond): punctuation (" " " ' ' ' , .) → 100% outdent; hyphen →
75%; en-dash → 50%; em-dash → 25%; A T V W Y → 20%; C O → 10%. A quantified
rule-of-thumb set for exactly the "deviate from the margin so the margin
looks straight" task. Mark as compiled/community rather than single-maker.

### 3.4 Kern On (Tim Ahrens) — the kerning judgment as software
**Status:** MAKER-ATTRIBUTED (vendor/Glyphs announcement) · **Link:**
https://glyphsapp.com/news/kern-on-released (verified)

Ahrens' kerning assistant for Glyphs: "It will ask you a few smart questions
about your kerning and then 'kerns on' for you… Once Kern On knows what you
want, it takes care of both the kern pairs and the kerning groups in a highly
optimized way." The designer's perceptual spacing decisions, captured from a
few manual examples and extrapolated across the font — craft judgment
formalized as a tool. (Exact extrapolation method not verified from this
announcement; described at the level the maker claims.)

## 4. Cross-cutting observations for the project

1. **Round 1's scarcity finding is confirmed and explained.** Practitioners
   describe corrections qualitatively in case studies; numbers surface in
   forums (TypeDrawers), software docs (Adobe), and compiled tables
   (Wikipedia) — not in brand case studies. Ahrens supplies the historical
   cause: the craft was transmitted by demonstration, not writing.
2. **A recurring maker-attested task: symbol–wordmark weight matching.** Both
   NBC (Tinker vs. peacock feathers) and Airbnb (wordmark vs. Bélo) describe
   tuning stroke weight, spacing, and curvature so two heterogeneous elements
   *feel balanced* together. This is a concrete, bounded version of the
   weight-function problem: equalizing w across two pixel groups by adjusting
   weight/spacing/curvature — and practitioners do it entirely by eye.
3. **The trade's anti-formula consensus is explicit and multi-voiced.**
   Schwartz (via Ahrens): "a range of correct answers, each appropriate to its
   own situation"; TypeDrawers: "increase the overshoot until it's noticeable,
   than back it off a bit"; Sowersby: continuous black/white balance testing.
   The practitioner's instrument is calibrated judgment, and they say so —
   which is itself data about the formula question (cf. Round 1 §2: the eye
   *is* the instrument).
4. **Where numbers do exist, they are ranges and stratifications, not
   constants:** overshoot 1–3% (text O), 2–4% of x-height, 3–8% (display),
   4–10 units/1000 UPM; OMA outdents 10–100% by glyph class; Helvetica's
   measured two-tier overshoot structure. The pattern is consistent with
   Round 1's verdict: contextual, relational weights — real, but parameterized.
